How we built this site

We rebuilt our own site by hand on Netlify for full ownership, faster changes, and a look that felt like us. We also ended up with a binned design, a colour pulled off a lime, an AI we'll own up to, and a bug that's still live. Here's what the rebuild taught us.

Sophie & Chris · June 2026 · 6 min read

You're reading this on the site it's about, which felt like the right place to start. We spend most of our time helping other people's brands look and sound like themselves, so our own site sat near the bottom of the list for a while. This is the working out of how it came together, not the tidy case study, and it's the first of these Fieldnotes.

We left the builder for a reason

The first version lived in Framer. It's quick, design-native, and easy to like, and for a while it did the job.

Two things wore us down. The copy kept drifting into a marketing voice neither of us talks in, lines like "we keep it moving" that perform energy rather than have any. And the site started bending around the tool instead of the other way round: small changes got awkward, patterns got harder to reuse, and at no point did we actually own the thing we were building.

Rebuilding by hand gave us three things worth the hassle:

  • Full ownership of the code.
  • Faster changes once the foundations were down.
  • No gap between what we designed and what shipped.

It wasn't the fast route. It did let us obsess over details a template would never have let us near.

Our first direction looked good and was still wrong

The biggest surprise was how easily "nice" passes for "right". Our first direction was soft and organic: rounded shapes, earthy colours, natural type. None of it was bad on its own. Together it felt like someone else's studio.

It wasn't a quality problem, it was a positioning one. We work mostly with tech and SaaS, so the site had to feel like we speak that language. But a fair amount of our work is for businesses that aren't software companies, and we didn't want any of them landing here and assuming it wasn't for them. So we kept the warmth and dropped the softness, kept the personality and cut the decoration, and ended up somewhere darker and more structured without turning into another anonymous startup site.

Throwing away finished work stings. We did it because we wanted a site we'd actually stand behind.

A lime decided our palette

The colours came from things we already had, not a generator. Green (#3DDC84) was there from the start. The gold (#F6D764) is lifted straight off a lime in one of our hero photos. Lavender (#B8A9E8) turned up later as a quieter third, for hover states and a couple of footer easter eggs.

A palette on its own isn't a brand, though. What turns it into one is using it the same way every time: gold for the things you click, green for identity and status, lavender in the background. The colours only became useful once they were rules.

Why we landed on the pixel effect

The homepage effects took more goes than anything else. Early versions looked impressive but disconnected. Some were too playful. Some sat too close to things we'd seen elsewhere.

We ended up with the pixelated bubble because it did a few jobs at once: it added movement without taking over the page, it felt digital without leaning on the usual tech clichés, and it tied the sections together. The point was never to add an effect. It was to find a visual language that backed up the positioning.

The About photo took the most fiddling. The first version shattered and scattered the photo on hover. We didn't like it, so we flipped the whole thing, and now it starts as a pixelated blob and clears as you move your cursor over it. We gave the hero and the photo the same physics so they read as one idea rather than two separate tricks.

Honesty, since that's the point of these notes: the hero effect still doesn't render properly on the right-hand side of the page. It's a canvas sizing thing, it's on the list, and you may well spot it before we fix it.

Yes, we used Claude Code

We should say this plainly, since being straight with you is the whole point of these notes. We built the site with Claude Code, an AI coding tool, with the two of us directing it. We're designers first, and on our own we'd probably have stayed in a builder rather than attempt a coded site at all. This is what made hand-coding realistic for us.

The honest workflow was less "AI builds the site" and more: it writes a version, we test it, we break it, we rewrite the parts it got wrong, and round again. It got plenty wrong. At one point it left a stray closing tag that quietly broke the layout of our contact page, the kind of thing you only catch by looking. It made us faster. It didn't make the decisions.

So the honest version is this: the tool handles the part that used to stop us, the code. It doesn't hand you taste or judgement, and you have to check its work. We're happy to say we used it. Pretending otherwise would be exactly the kind of polish we're trying to avoid.

The honest numbers

The figures, because that's why we're writing this down:

  • Time: somewhere around 40 hours, give or take, spread over a few weeks of evenings.
  • Domain: £__ a year with Blacknight, an Irish registrar a friend pointed us to, cheaper than the big-name option we were about to use.
  • Hosting: £0 (Netlify).
  • Tools: £0 in software, plus Claude Code and our own time.

Most of those hours went into the design, the copy, and the effects, not the code underneath. We could have shipped faster. We chose to sweat the detail, down to swapping the placeholder emoji on the service cards for a proper icon set, because for a studio the site is the work.

Building it ourselves changed how we work

The less obvious payoff shows up after launch. Small updates stopped feeling expensive. Ideas became easy to test. The site turned into something we tinker with rather than something we avoid touching, which is the opposite of how the builder version felt by the end. We wanted something that grows with the business, not another platform migration in eighteen months. Owning it turned out to be as much about how we behave as about the code.

What we'd do differently

  • Sort the positioning first. The visual calls get much easier once the strategic ones are made. We learned that by building the wrong direction first.
  • Write the words before the layout. We kept designing boxes before we knew what went in them, then reshaping them when the copy arrived.
  • Decide sooner. Most of our dithering came from leaving too many routes open.
  • Build for maintenance, not launch day. The real work starts once it's live.
  • Expect to iterate. The first good version is rarely the last, and the hero bug is proof.

Would we do it again?

Yes, binned design and all. If you care about craft and you'll keep your hands on the site, hand-coding on Netlify is cheap, quick to run, and entirely yours. If you'd rather move fast and never think about it again, a builder is very good at exactly that. We turned out to be the first sort.

This is the first of these Fieldnotes. We'll do more on the calls behind our work, the tools we use, and what it's like running a small studio in Guernsey. If there's something you'd want us to pull apart, tell us.
